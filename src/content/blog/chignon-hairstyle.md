The chignon is the updo that never dates: hair gathered low, twisted or coiled, and pinned flat at the nape of the neck — the name comes from the French *chignon du cou*, "the nape of the neck", which is exactly where it sits. It reads formal without looking laboured, keeps every strand off the face and shoulders, and holds its shape for a full day in a way looser updos rarely manage.

It is also the style most often confused with a low bun, and the two are built differently — which is why a chignon attempted like a bun tends to collapse by lunchtime. Below: what makes a chignon a chignon, the pins that make it hold, the full step-by-step, four variations, and an honest word on how short is too short.

## 1. What Is a Chignon?

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-01.webp" alt="Woman with dark brown hair in a classic low chignon coiled at the nape, seen from behind in an elegant dark green evening dress" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

A chignon is defined by two things: position and construction. The position is fixed — the nape, low at the back of the neck, never the crown. The construction is a twist or a coil: the lengths are gathered into a low ponytail, twisted along themselves or rolled, then wound flat against the head and pinned, with every end tucked out of sight. The finished shape is elongated and close to the head rather than round and perched.

Because the weight sits low and spread across the nape, a chignon stays comfortable for hours — nothing pulls at one point of the scalp. It has been worn for centuries, from ballet dancers to French evening dress, because the silhouette is simple: smooth head, neat coil, bare neck. If your hair reaches your shoulders or beyond, you already have everything the classic version needs.

## 2. Chignon vs Low Bun: The Difference That Matters

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-06.webp" alt="Close-up of U-shaped pins pushed into the edge of a coiled chignon against the head at the nape" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

A bun is built by wrapping: hair is wound around a central point — or around a donut — into a rounded knot, and it can sit anywhere from the crown to the nape. Our [low bun hairstyles](/hairstyles/updos-buns/low-bun-hairstyles/) guide builds them exactly that way, looping and wrapping the lengths into a soft, round shape. A chignon is built by twisting and coiling instead: the ponytail is twisted along its own length first, then wound flat in a spiral and sewn down with pins. The result lies flatter, stretches wider across the nape, and hides its ends inside the coil rather than under a wrap.

The practical differences follow from that. A chignon needs pins, not a second elastic; it reads more formal than a bun; and it holds layered or slippery hair better, because the twist locks the layers together. The [slick back bun](/hairstyles/updos-buns/slick-back-bun/) sits at the opposite end again — the same low placement is possible, but the finish there is all about the glassy surface, while a chignon's finish is about the coil.

## 3. The Tools: U-Pins Do the Holding, Grips Do the Tidying

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-02.webp" alt="Flat lay of chignon styling tools on linen: U-shaped hair pins, bobby grips, a small elastic, a boar-bristle brush, texture spray and a fine-tooth comb" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The pin confusion is where most chignons fail, because the two common pins do opposite jobs. **U-pins** (the open, two-pronged hair pins) are the structural tool: they do not clamp, they anchor. A U-pin woven into the coil and turned against the scalp hair holds a large amount of hair without squashing it flat — four to six can hold an entire chignon. **Bobby grips** (kirby grips) clamp small sections shut — perfect for tucking in a stray end, pinning a face-framing piece or securing the very base, but far too weak to carry the coil itself. A chignon pinned only with grips slides out within the hour; that is a tool problem, not a hair problem.

Beyond the pins you need very little: one small elastic for the ponytail base, a brush, and something for texture — dry shampoo or texture spray. Hairspray is a finishing touch, not a structural one. If your pins show, they are too long for your coil or not pushed fully home — bury them completely.

## 4. Prep: Slippery Hair Is the Enemy

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-03.webp" alt="Woman gathering brushed hair into a smooth low ponytail at the nape, the base step of a chignon" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

A chignon holds by friction, and freshly washed hair has almost none. Second-day hair is the easiest starting point — a trace of natural oil gives the twist something to grip. If your hair is clean, fine or naturally silky, build the friction in: mist texture spray or work dry shampoo through the lengths (not just the roots), then lightly backcomb the ponytail once it is tied — tease the underside only, so the surface stays smooth while the inside becomes a grippy core pins can bite into. A pinch of volumising powder does the same job on very fine hair.

What not to do: load the lengths with oil or serum first. Smoothing products are for the finished surface and the hairline, applied at the end in tiny amounts. Hair prepped slippery will unwind itself no matter how well you pin it.

## 5. The Classic Low Chignon, Step by Step

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-04.webp" alt="Woman twisting a low ponytail firmly along its length before coiling it into a chignon" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

**Step 1: Brush and part.** Brush the hair smooth and set your parting — centre, side, or none, with the hair swept straight back. The classic chignon is traditionally parting-free or centre-parted; a side part softens it.

**Step 2: The low ponytail.** Gather the hair into a ponytail at the nape, sitting just above the hairline, and secure it with the small elastic. Smooth, but not scalp-tight — the coil still has to be able to turn.

**Step 3: Add grip.** If your hair is fine, backcomb the ponytail lightly now, or work in a little texture spray. This is the last moment you can change the ponytail's texture.

**Step 4: Twist.** Twist the ponytail firmly along its length, always in the same direction, until it feels tight and starts to curl back on itself.

**Step 5: Coil.** Wind the twist flat against the head in a spiral around the ponytail base, keeping it low and wide rather than tall, and tuck the ends underneath the coil.

**Step 6: Pin and finish.** Pin the coil down as the next section explains, then smooth the surface with the brush, tidy the hairline, and finish with a light mist of hairspray at arm's length.

## 6. Pin Placement: Into the Coil, Against the Head

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-05.webp" alt="Woman coiling a twisted ponytail flat against the nape in a spiral to form a chignon" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Pin as if you were sewing the coil to your head — because that is literally the job. Take a U-pin, push it into the outer edge of the coil so it catches a good bite of coiled hair, then angle it down and push it in against the scalp, catching the hair growing from the head underneath. The pin should travel through both layers; that sandwich is what holds. Work around the coil — top, both sides, underneath — with four to six pins, and tuck loose ends in with a grip as you find them.

Test the hold honestly: shake your head, nod, look down. Wherever the coil moves, it needs one more pin, placed at the point of movement rather than randomly. A well-pinned chignon feels like nothing at all; if a pin digs, slide it out and re-place it slightly deeper into the coil.

## 7. Four Variations Worth Knowing

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-07.webp" alt="Ballerina with sleek glossy hair in a polished low chignon at the nape with a smooth, formal finish" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

**Sleek ballerina.** The formal extreme: brush a little styling cream or light gel through before the ponytail, twist extra tightly, and coil as flat as you can. A fine hair net over the finished coil, pinned at the edges, is the dancer's trick for a finish that survives an entire evening. Best on oval and heart-shaped faces, and the version for black-tie dress codes and all-day wedding photographs.

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-08.webp" alt="Woman with a soft, romantic messy chignon at the nape and loose face-framing strands" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

**Soft romantic.** The wedding-guest favourite: keep the texture in — curl or wave the hair loosely first if it is very straight, coil loosely rather than tightly, then pull a few strands free at the front and let the odd end escape at the nape. Face-framing pieces soften round and square faces, and the looseness reads modern rather than severe. Finish with fingers, not a brush.

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-09.webp" alt="Woman with an elegant side chignon positioned low behind one ear with a smooth twisted finish" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

**Side chignon.** Gather the ponytail low behind one ear instead of at the centre of the nape, then twist and coil as normal. Paired with a deep side part it is the most flattering version for strong jawlines and one-shoulder or asymmetric dresses, and it photographs beautifully, because the coil shows over one shoulder.

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-10.webp" alt="Woman with a braided chignon at the nape, a three-strand braid coiled and pinned into an updo" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

**Braided chignon.** Braid the ponytail — a plain three-strand braid is enough — then coil the braid and pin it. The braid's texture grips the pins far better than loose hair, hides short layers that would otherwise poke out, and suits anyone whose chignon keeps unravelling. It is the same braid-first logic as our [braided ponytail hairstyles](/hairstyles/updos-buns/braided-ponytail-hairstyles/), wound up instead of left hanging.

## 8. Shorter Hair: The Honest Length Guide and the Loop Trick

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/chignon-hairstyle/chignon-hairstyle-03.webp" alt="Woman gathering brushed hair into a smooth low ponytail at the nape, the base step of a chignon" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The classic chignon needs about shoulder-length hair: enough ponytail to twist, coil once around its base, and tuck the ends under. Much shorter and the coil cannot close — no amount of pinning fixes a lack of length.

Between chin and shoulder length, use the loop trick instead. Tie the low ponytail, but on the last wrap of the elastic pull the hair only halfway through, leaving a small loop with the ends pointing down. Fan the loop slightly, wrap the loose ends around the elastic base, and pin everything flat. It reads as a small, neat chignon and holds far better than a forced coil. Above chin length, be honest with yourself: a true chignon is not available, but a [claw clip hairstyle](/hairstyles/updos-buns/claw-clip-hairstyles/) twist gives the same swept-up, off-the-nape effect in ten seconds.

## Making It Last — and Taking It Down

A chignon pinned into textured hair should last a full working day or an entire wedding without attention. Carry two spare U-pins in your bag anyway; if the coil loosens, one extra pin at the point of movement restores it — never take the whole style down to fix it. For work, keep the surface smooth and the coil compact; for evening, the same base takes a jewelled pin or a ribbon woven through the coil without rebuilding anything.

Take it down in reverse: pins out first, elastic second, then brush gently from the ends upwards. Backcombing brushes out easiest after a light mist of conditioner or detangler — dragging a brush through teased, sprayed hair dry is how ends snap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does hair need to be for a chignon?

Around shoulder length for the classic twist-and-coil version — you need enough ponytail to twist, wind once around its base and tuck the ends underneath. Chin-to-shoulder hair can use the loop trick described above. Above chin length there simply is not enough hair to form a coil, and a clip twist is the honest alternative.

### Why does my chignon keep falling out?

Almost always one of three causes: grips doing a U-pin's job, hair that is too clean and slippery to hold friction, or pins placed into the coil alone without catching the hair against the head. Fix the texture first, pin through both layers, and test with a head shake — then add pins where it moves, not where it looks thin.

### U-pins or bobby grips — which should I buy?

Both, because they do different jobs: U-pins anchor the coil, grips tidy ends, edges and face-framing pieces. If you only buy one pack, buy U-pins roughly the width of your finished coil — they are the structural tool, and a chignon built on grips alone will slide out. Match the colour loosely to your hair so the pins disappear.

### Can you do a chignon on curly or wavy hair?

Yes — texture is an advantage here, because curly hair gives pins far more to grip. Gather it with your fingers and a little cream rather than brushing it dry (which only creates frizz), and twist gently. The soft romantic version suits curls best; straighten first only if you specifically want the sleek ballerina finish.

### How do I stop a chignon looking dated or severe?

Loosen it. Severity comes from over-smoothing, not from the shape itself: keep some texture in the lengths, pull out a few face-framing strands, choose the side or romantic version over the ballerina coil, and go easy on the hairspray. A modern accessory — a simple metal pin, a ribbon — also brings it straight up to date.

### How long does a chignon take to do?

About five minutes once you have done it a few times: the classic version is six steps, and only the pinning takes any real care. Expect ten to fifteen minutes on your first attempts, mostly spent on pin placement. A well-pinned one then holds all day without touch-ups.
