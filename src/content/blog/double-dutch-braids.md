Double Dutch braids — two Dutch braids, one down each side of a centre parting, sometimes called boxer braids — are the style most people actually want when they search for a Dutch braid. They frame the face symmetrically, stay put all day, and look far more complicated than they are. The honest catch: this tutorial assumes you can already braid a single Dutch braid. The under-crossing technique is the whole foundation, and it is taught step by step in our [Dutch braid tutorial](/hairstyles/braids/how-to-dutch-braid/). What this article teaches is everything the single braid does not: the parting, working both sides to match, keeping the tension even, and the finishes that turn two braids into five different styles.

That division of labour is good news. Two smaller braids are more forgiving than one large centre braid — the sections are smaller, the track on each side is shorter, and each braid sits where your hands can actually reach it. Most people find double Dutch braids easier on their own hair than the single version.

## What You Need

- A brush or wide-tooth comb, plus a tail comb for the parting (the tail comb is not optional here — the parting is the style)
- Two small elastics, plus a sectioning clip or a third elastic to hold the waiting side
- A hand mirror or phone camera to check the back
- Optional but helpful: a little styling cream or a water mister. Second-day hair grips best; freshly washed hair slips out of the second braid while you work on the first.

## First, the Ground Rule: One Braid Before Two

A Dutch braid is a French braid turned inside out: each side section crosses **under** the centre instead of over it, so the braid sits on top of the hair as a raised ridge. That single habit — under, not over — is all the technique there is, and the [single Dutch braid tutorial](/hairstyles/braids/how-to-dutch-braid/) walks through it strand by strand, including the five beginner mistakes. Learn it there first, even if only on a practice head or a patient friend. Everything below is choreography built on top of that one move: where to part, how to mirror, and how hard to pull. If your hands still slip into over-crossing, two braids will just give you two mismatched ones.

## Step by Step: Double Dutch Braids

### 1. Part the hair dead centre

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/double-dutch-braids/double-dutch-braids-02.webp" alt="Drawing a straight centre parting with a tail comb before braiding" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Brush all the hair straight back, then use the tail of the comb to draw the parting in one confident line from the centre of the hairline to the nape. Work in front of a mirror and check the back with a hand mirror — a parting that drifts half an inch off centre at the crown will make one braid visibly thicker than the other, and no amount of careful braiding fixes it afterwards. Once the line is clean, clip one entire side up and out of the way. Never braid with the other half hanging loose: stray hair from the waiting side creeps across the parting and gets woven into the wrong braid.

### 2. Braid the first side

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/double-dutch-braids/double-dutch-braids-03.webp" alt="Braiding the first Dutch braid while the other side is clipped away" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

On the free side, take the starting section at the hairline — smaller than for a single braid, roughly two inches wide — and begin exactly as the single tutorial teaches: two or three plain under-crosses to anchor, then add a small slice of hair before each cross, working down past the ear to the nape and finishing as a plain three-strand braid to the ends. Keep your hands close to the scalp and aim the braid along a line that runs from the hairline, past the top of the ear, to the centre of the nape. Secure with an elastic, then — before you touch the other side — note two things: how high the braid started, and how big your added slices were. The second braid has to copy both.

### 3. Match the second side

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/double-dutch-braids/double-dutch-braids-04.webp" alt="Two Dutch braids reaching the nape at the same height on both sides" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Unclip the waiting side and start the second braid at the mirror-image point: the same height on the hairline, the same width of starting section, the same size of added slices. This matching is the real skill of double Dutch braids. Braid to the nape, then stop and compare before finishing the lengths — hold both braids at the nape and check in the mirror that they arrive at the same height and sit at the same angle. If one side started higher or took bigger slices, it will run out of loose hair sooner and sit tighter. It is far easier to undo and redo one braid at this point than to disguise a mismatch later, and a redo takes two minutes.

### 4. Finish the lengths and check the pair

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/double-dutch-braids/double-dutch-braids-01.webp" alt="Finished double Dutch braids running down either side of a centre parting" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Continue both braids as plain three-strand braids to the ends and secure them at the same point on each side — elastics at uneven heights are the small detail that makes a good pair look careless. Do a final turn in the mirror: the parting should read as one straight line, the ridges should sit at matching angles like a pair of quotation marks, and neither braid should pull harder at the temple than the other. A gentle tug on the tighter side's first few crosses evens out most small differences in feel.

### 5. Pancake both braids — evenly

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/double-dutch-braids/double-dutch-braids-05.webp" alt="Pancaking a Dutch braid by gently pulling the outer edges of each segment" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Pancaking — gently pulling the outer edges of each segment outward to flatten and widen the braid — is the standard double Dutch finish, and the raised ridge gives you more to work with than any other braid. The rule for a pair is symmetry: work from the bottom up on one braid, count roughly how much you pulled each segment, then repeat the same amount on the other side. Pancake one side enthusiastically and the other barely, and you will have one thick braid and one thin one. Pull a little at a time on both, stepping back to compare as you go.

## Five Finishes to Try

### Double Dutch into buns

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/double-dutch-braids/double-dutch-braids-06.webp" alt="Double Dutch braids twisted up into two space buns" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Braid both sides only as far as the nape, secure each with an elastic, then twist each loose length into a bun and pin or elastic it in place. Because the braids already hold the hair flat and tidy against the head, these buns stay put far better than unbraided ones — it is the finish that survives a full day, a festival, or a dance class. Our [space buns](/hairstyles/updos-buns/space-buns/) tutorial covers the bun shapes themselves if you want more than a simple twist.

### Double Dutch into ponytails

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/double-dutch-braids/double-dutch-braids-07.webp" alt="Double Dutch braids flowing into two high ponytails" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Stop each braid where you want the ponytail to sit — high at the crown for a sporty finish, low at the nape for a softer one — secure with a proper hair tie rather than a small elastic, and leave the lengths loose. The braided section does the decorative work and the ponytails do the moving. This is also the most forgiving finish for beginners, because it hides the braid ends completely.

### Half-up double Dutch

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/double-dutch-braids/double-dutch-braids-08.webp" alt="Half-up double Dutch braids with the rest of the hair worn loose" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Instead of parting all the way to the nape, take only the top section of hair on each side — roughly everything above the ears — and Dutch braid those, securing them at the crown or letting them meet at the back, while the underneath hair stays loose. It gives you the double Dutch look with half the commitment, takes about five minutes, and is the version that works on the widest range of lengths, including bobs.

### Double Dutch for workouts

The style's sporting reputation is earned: braided flat against the head, double Dutch braids keep hair off the face and neck, do not swing like a ponytail, and survive running, lifting and most training without a single pin. Two adjustments make them genuinely workout-proof. Braid slightly tighter than usual, particularly the first few crosses at the hairline, and use proper small elastics rather than decorative ones. Then leave the pancaking out — a sleek, unpancaked braid has nothing to catch or loosen. Braided in the morning, they will also see you through the rest of the day, which is precisely why so many people learn them for the gym and end up wearing them everywhere.

### Double Dutch on shorter hair — honestly

Full double Dutch braids, hairline to ends, need roughly shoulder-length hair. Above that, the lengths run out before the braid reaches the nape, the ends escape, and the style unravels within the hour — that is a geometry problem, not a skill problem, and tighter braiding does not solve it. What genuinely works on chin- to shoulder-length hair is the half-up version above: the braids travel a much shorter distance, so shorter hair can complete them, and the loose hair below hides any escaping ends. On a bob, keep the braids small and close to the parting and expect to tidy them by early evening.

## Keeping Both Sides Even: 4 Fixes

1. **One braid is thicker than the other.** The parting was off centre, or the slices on one side were bigger. Re-check the parting in the back mirror first — it is the cause more often than the braiding.
2. **One braid sits higher than the other.** The starting sections began at different heights. Compare the pair at the nape before finishing the lengths, and redo the side that started wrong.
3. **One braid feels tighter.** Your dominant hand pulls harder. Consciously soften your grip on that side, and tighten the looser braid gently after each full cycle instead of pulling the tight one looser.
4. **The parting disappears by the nape.** Hair crept across from the waiting side. Clip the second side away properly, and re-draw the bottom of the parting with the tail comb before starting the second braid.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are double Dutch braids the same as boxer braids?

Yes — they are the same style with two names. "Boxer braids" took hold because the style is popular with fighters and athletes for training; "double Dutch braids" describes the technique, two Dutch braids either side of a centre parting. If a tutorial uses either name, the steps are identical.

### Do I need to learn the single Dutch braid first?

It is strongly recommended. Double Dutch braids use exactly the same under-crossing technique, just on two smaller sections — so any wobble in the basic move shows up twice. An hour with the single braid first, even on someone else's hair, makes the double version dramatically easier.

### Why is one of my braids always tighter than the other?

Almost everyone's dominant hand pulls harder and takes slightly bigger slices. The fix is awareness rather than force: soften the grip on your strong side, and on the weaker side, give the braid a gentle firming tug after each full cycle. Checking the pair at the nape, before finishing the lengths, catches the difference while it is still cheap to redo.

### How long does hair need to be for double Dutch braids?

About shoulder length for the full version, braided from hairline to ends — below that, the hair runs out before the braid reaches the nape. The half-up version works from roughly chin length, because each braid travels a much shorter distance and the loose hair underneath hides the ends.

### How do I keep the centre parting straight all the way down?

Draw it in one line with a tail comb before you start, check the back with a hand mirror, and clip the waiting side fully out of the way so nothing creeps across. If the bottom of the parting has blurred by the time you start the second braid, simply re-draw the last few inches — the braids will cover the join.

### How long do double Dutch braids last?

Styled in the morning, a neat pair lasts the day and will survive a workout. Slept in, they soften into a looser, messier version that many people happily wear for a second day, though the parting and hairline will look lived-in. They are a one- to two-day style, not a long-wear protective style — take them out, brush through gently, and let the hair rest.

## Final Thoughts

Double Dutch braids are not a new technique — they are the [single Dutch braid](/hairstyles/braids/how-to-dutch-braid/) with a parting, a mirror, and a little discipline about matching. Get the parting straight, copy your own measurements from one side to the other, pancake evenly, and the pair will look deliberate every time. Start with the ponytail finish while you are learning; it forgives everything.
