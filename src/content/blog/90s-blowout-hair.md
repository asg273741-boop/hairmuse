The 90s blowout defined a decade of supermodels: hair lifted high at the roots, lengths smooth and glossy, ends bent into that unmistakable flick, and a fringe that sweeps rather than hangs. It is not a curl style and it is not poker-straight either — it is volume and bend, held together by heat, tension and a proper cool-down.

There are three honest routes to it, and this tutorial covers all of them: the **round brush and hairdryer** method (the real technique, and the one that looks most like the original), **velcro rollers after a rough blow-dry** (less arm work, nearly the same finish), and a **heatless overnight** version (the softest result — more on its limits below). Whichever route you choose, the prep and the finishing rules are the same.

## 1. What Makes a Blowout "90s"

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/90s-blowout-hair/90s-blowout-hair-01.webp" alt="Woman with a finished 90s blowout — big root volume, glossy lengths and flicked-out ends, in front of a dressing-room mirror" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Four things separate a 90s blowout from an ordinary blow-dry. **Root lift**: the hair leaves the scalp at an angle, not flat against it. **Smooth, stretched lengths**: the hair is pulled taut as it dries, which is where the gloss comes from. **Bent ends**: the last few centimetres curve or flick — under, out, or alternating — rather than hanging straight. And **movement**: the finished hair is bouncy and swishy, not helmet-set. Miss one of the four and the result reads as an ordinary blow-dry.

## 2. Prep: Mousse, Heat Protectant and the 80% Rule

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/90s-blowout-hair/90s-blowout-hair-03.webp" alt="Woman working mousse through damp, freshly washed hair before starting her blowout" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Start on freshly washed hair — a blowout built on day-three roots never lifts properly. Work heat protectant through first, then a golf-ball-sized amount of volumising mousse from roots to ends (half that on fine hair; skip it and fine hair will drop within the hour). Mousse is not optional decoration here: it is the scaffolding the volume stands on.

Then rough-dry — dryer only, no brush — until the hair is about **80% dry**: damp to the touch, no longer dripping or cold-wet. Start brush-work on soaking hair and you will be there twice as long with half the smoothness; start on bone-dry hair and the shape has already set flat before you begin. The brush work has to happen in that damp window where hair is still persuadable.

## 3. Route One: Round Brush and Hairdryer (The Real Technique)

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/90s-blowout-hair/90s-blowout-hair-04.webp" alt="Close-up of a woman blow-drying a taut section of hair wrapped around a large round brush, dryer nozzle angled down the shaft" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

This is the salon method, and it rests on three things: **tension, heat direction, and the cool shot.**

First, section. Clip everything up except a horizontal row at the nape, and work upwards in rows, then the sides, then the crown strip last. Sections should be no wider than your brush and thin enough to see through — thick sections stay damp and flat at the root.

Take one section, place a large round brush (45–55 mm for the classic bend; bigger for length, smaller for short hair) underneath at the roots, and pull the section **up and out** from the head as you dry — that upward angle is the root lift being built in. Keep real tension on the brush, the way you would pull a ribbon straight over a scissor blade. Aim the dryer nozzle **down the hair shaft**, from roots towards ends, following the brush: air blown up the shaft roughs the surface and makes frizz, air blown down it is what produces the glossy, stretched finish. Roll the brush slowly through the ends several times to bend them, then — the step everyone skips — hold the section wound on the brush and give it five to ten seconds of **cool air**. Heat shapes hair; cooling is what makes it keep the shape. Release, pin or clip the section loosely if you like, and move on.

Work through every section the same way. Your arms will complain the first few times — that is normal, and exactly why route two exists.

## 4. The Fringe Finish

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/90s-blowout-hair/90s-blowout-hair-06.webp" alt="Close-up of a woman blow-drying her fringe with a round brush, pulling it forwards and down so it curves into a sweep" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The fringe (or the face-framing front layers, if you do not wear one) makes or breaks the 90s read, so do it last, while it is still in that damp window — re-wet it with a spray bottle if it has dried. For a full fringe, place a medium round brush underneath, pull the hair **forwards and down** over the forehead as you dry, and roll the ends under: it should land in a curved sweep, not a straight curtain and not a flick. For curtain-style fronts, dry each side away from the face, rolling the brush backwards, so the front pieces open outwards like the original supermodel fringe. Cool-shot it before you let go — the front drops fastest of all, and it needs the set more than any other section.

## 5. Route Two: Rough Dry, Then Velcro Rollers

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/90s-blowout-hair/90s-blowout-hair-05.webp" alt="Woman with large velcro rollers set through her hair after a rough blow-dry, rolled away from the face on top" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

If the round brush defeats you — or your arms — this route gets you most of the way there. Rough-dry with the dryer and your fingers, lifting the roots with your fingertips as you go, until the hair is fully dry and warm. Then roll it all onto large and jumbo velcro rollers while it is still warm: away from the face along the crown strip, downwards at the sides and back. Leave them in for 20–30 minutes while the hair cools completely, unroll gently in reverse, and finish exactly as below.

Honestly, the trade-off: the roots lift just as well, but the lengths are a touch less stretched and glossy than the brush method, because nothing pulled them taut under heat. For the full method — roller sizes by length, rolling direction, setting times and frizz-free removal — our [velcro rollers tutorial](/hairstyles/waves-curls/velcro-rollers/) is the deep-dive.

## 6. Route Three: The Heatless Overnight Version

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/90s-blowout-hair/90s-blowout-hair-07.webp" alt="Woman in the evening with her hair wrapped around a satin robe belt tied on top of her head, ready for overnight heatless styling" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The heatless version uses the overnight wrap you may already know from our [robe belt curls](/hairstyles/waves-curls/robe-belt-curls/): hair wound around a robe belt (or large soft rollers) before bed, on hair that is barely damp with a little mousse through it. In the morning, unwind, shake the roots out with your fingertips and smooth the surface with your palms.

Be clear-eyed about what you get. This route produces soft, loose waves with some body — pretty, but it is not the full 90s blowout. There is no tension and no heat, so the lengths are less smooth, the root lift is modest, and the flicked ends are more of a gentle bend. Choose it for zero heat, or as a next-day base to refresh; do not choose it if the glossy supermodel finish is the whole point.

## 7. Short Hair and Long Hair Adjustments

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/90s-blowout-hair/90s-blowout-hair-08.webp" alt="Woman with short bob-length hair wearing a big, voluminous 90s blowout with flicked-out ends, side profile" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

**Short hair** (bobs and lobs) does the 90s blowout brilliantly — arguably better than long hair, because the ends have less weight pulling the bend out. Drop a brush size (32–43 mm), roll the ends with real intent so the flick shows, and expect the whole job to take ten minutes. The risk at this length is over-rounding into a bubble shape: dry the underneath layers straighter and save the big bend for the top layer and the ends.

**Long hair** needs the opposite patience. Use the largest brush you own, work in thinner sections than feels reasonable, and accept that the bend will live mostly in the last third — gravity wins at the roots-to-mid-lengths on very long hair. Clipping each finished section into a loose roll while it cools makes a visible difference at this length, as does finishing route one with a few jumbo rollers through the crown from route two.

## 8. Fix Flat Roots and Droopy Ends, and Make It Last

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/90s-blowout-hair/90s-blowout-hair-02.webp" alt="Back view of a woman shaking out a finished 90s blowout, showing the volume through the crown and the bouncing curved ends" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Finish every route the same way: head upside down, fingertips in at the roots, shake. Rake the lengths with fingers only — a brush now turns bounce into fluff — and mist flexible-hold hairspray at arm's length.

**Flat roots** are usually one of three things: the section was dried pointing down instead of up and out; the hair was already dry before the brush reached it; or product is sitting at the root weighing it down (serum and oil belong on the ends only). The rescue: re-dampen just the roots, re-dry them upwards, cool-shot, or cheat with one jumbo roller in the crown for fifteen minutes.

**Droopy ends** mean the bend never set — the ends were dried but not cooled on the brush. Re-roll the last few centimetres on a warm brush, cool-shot properly this time, and they will hold.

And the honest lifespan: a 90s blowout is at its best for **hours to a day**. Humidity is its enemy — on a damp day even a perfect blowout can soften by lunchtime. Sleep with the hair piled loosely on top of your head in a scrunchie or wrapped in a silk scarf, and day two becomes a softer, looser version rather than a collapse; refresh the front and crown only, and leave the rest alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a 90s blowout take to do?

Route one (round brush) takes 25–40 minutes once you have the technique, longer while you are learning. The velcro roller route is about 10 minutes of rough-drying and rolling plus 20–30 minutes of setting time. The heatless route takes 10 minutes in the evening and 5 in the morning.

### Why does my blowout fall flat within an hour?

Almost always the cool-down. Hair shaped with heat but released while still warm relaxes straight back; each section needs to cool on the brush or in a roller before it is let go. The other usual causes: hair too dry to reshape, and no mousse for hold.

### Can I get a 90s blowout on fine hair?

Yes — fine hair lifts well because it is light. Use half the mousse you think you need, smaller sections, and a smaller brush so the hair wraps enough times to bend. Fine hair's problem is the opposite of thick hair's: the blowout forms easily but drops sooner, so the cool shot and a light hairspray finish matter more, not less.

### What round brush size do I need for a 90s blowout?

A large brush — 45–55 mm — for medium to long hair, because the 90s bend is a big, soft curve rather than a curl. Drop to 32–43 mm for bobs and short layers. Boar or mixed-bristle brushes grip and tension best; ceramic barrels dry faster but grip less.

### Is the heatless version as good as the blow-dryer version?

No, and it is worth saying plainly. The overnight wrap gives soft waves and some body, but without tension and heat the lengths are less smooth, the roots lift less, and the ends bend rather than flick. It is a good low-effort alternative and a good second-day base — it is not the same finish.

### How do I stop a blowout going frizzy?

Aim the dryer down the hair shaft, never up it; keep the nozzle a few centimetres from the brush; and make sure each section is completely dry before moving on — a section left 5% damp will frizz as it finishes drying on its own. A pea-sized amount of serum on the ends only, never the roots, finishes the surface.

## Final Thoughts

The 90s blowout looks like a product of glamour, but it is really a product of three unglamorous things: damp-enough hair, real tension, and the patience to let every section cool. Master those and the round brush route will give you the full supermodel finish; on the days they defeat you, the rollers are a respectable second.
