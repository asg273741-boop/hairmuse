Beach waves are the most requested wave and the most frequently misunderstood. They are not curls that failed, and they are not the glossy, uniform S-wave of a red carpet. A beach wave is deliberately unfinished: matte rather than shiny, separated into distinct pieces rather than brushed into one sheet, and — the detail that makes or breaks the look — the last couple of centimetres of every section are left straight. Those undone ends are what stop the style reading as "curled" and start it reading as "slept in salt water."

That looseness is also why beach waves frustrate people. The technique is mostly subtraction: less heat than you think, less product than you think, and less perfection than any other wave style asks for. Every method below follows the same three rules — alternate the direction, leave the ends out, and finish with texture rather than shine.

Below: what separates beach waves from Hollywood and mermaid waves, four ways to make them, how the approach changes by length, and the five mistakes that quietly ruin them.

## 1. What Beach Waves Actually Are

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/beach-waves/beach-waves-01.webp" alt="Close view of beach waves showing the matte finish, separated pieces and straight undone ends that define the style" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Three features define the style. First, the finish is matte: beach waves should look like hair that dried in sea air, not hair that was lacquered, so shine products and heavy oils work against it. Second, the waves are separated — you should be able to count individual pieces, each bending in its own direction. Third, the ends stay straight and a little piecey, which is why the style looks relaxed even when it took twenty minutes.

Set that against the two styles it gets confused with. [Hollywood glamour waves](/hairstyles/waves-curls/hollywood-glamour-waves/) are the opposite in every respect: glossy, uniform, all waves bending the same way and brushed through into one continuous S. [Mermaid waves](/hairstyles/waves-curls/mermaid-waves/) are a deep, regular crimp made with a waver — every ridge the same depth and spacing, more like a pattern than a texture. Beach waves sit at the undone end of the spectrum: irregular, flat in places, and better for looking slightly different every time you do them.

## 2. Method One: The Flat Iron

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/beach-waves/beach-waves-02.webp" alt="Flat iron being rocked back and forth through a section of hair to form a loose beach wave, the ends left out of the plates" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The flat iron gives the most authentic beach wave because it bends hair rather than coiling it, producing the flat, ribbon-like wave the style is named for. Work on dry hair with heat protectant, in sections roughly two fingers wide.

Clamp the section a few centimetres from the root. Then rock the iron as you glide: tip it toward you, slide down a few centimetres, tip it away, slide again — a slow seesaw all the way down. Keep the movement continuous; stopping creates a crease. Release before the ends so the last 2 cm pass through unbent or skip them entirely. Alternate the starting direction on every section — first section tips toward the face first, next section tips away — so neighbouring waves bend against each other instead of merging into one big curl.

Let every piece cool completely before you touch it. Then shake the roots with your fingertips and pull the waves apart gently. Do not brush; brushing is how flat-iron waves turn into a single fluffy mass.

## 3. Method Two: The Curling Wand

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/beach-waves/beach-waves-03.webp" alt="Hair being wrapped around a curling wand with the last two centimetres of the ends held out straight, away from the barrel" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

A wand — a barrel with no clamp — makes a rounder wave than a flat iron, so the technique has to compensate. Use a barrel around 25–32 mm; anything smaller makes spirals, not waves.

Hold the wand pointing downward and wrap the section around it flat against the barrel, not twisted like a rope — a flat wrap gives a wave, a twisted wrap gives a curl. Hold the last 2 cm of the ends out with your fingers so they never touch the heat. Five to eight seconds is enough for most hair; the wave should look almost too loose when it comes off the barrel, because it will relax further as it cools.

Direction is everything here. Wrap one section away from the face, the next toward it, all the way around the head. Uniform direction is the single most common reason wand waves come out looking like a pageant style instead of a beach style. Cool fully, then rake through once with wide-spread fingers and finish at the roots, not the lengths.

## 4. Method Three: Heatless Overnight Braids and Twists

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/beach-waves/beach-waves-04.webp" alt="Hair in two loose overnight braids being undone in the morning to reveal soft heatless beach waves" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Start on hair that is about 80 percent dry — fully wet hair sets too hard and kinks at the ties, fully dry hair barely takes the shape. Work a small amount of mousse or texture cream through the lengths first.

For loose waves, use two to four braids; for tighter, more defined waves, use more. Braid loosely and stop a few centimetres before the ends, securing with a soft tie — the unbraided ends dry straight, which conveniently matches the beach-wave rule. Twists and rope braids give a smoother, more spiral wave; standard three-strand braids give the flatter, crimped-at-the-edges wave that reads most like the real thing.

In the morning, undo gently and separate with fingers only. If you prefer a tool-free curl pattern with more spring, [sock curls](/hairstyles/waves-curls/sock-curls/) are the neighbouring technique — they trade the braid's flat wave for a rounder one.

## 5. Method Four: The Sea-Salt Scrunch for Naturally Wavy Hair

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/beach-waves/beach-waves-05.webp" alt="Hands scrunching naturally wavy hair upward with sea salt spray to encourage matte, separated beach waves" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

If your hair already has a wave pattern, this is the fastest route — you are amplifying texture, not creating it. Mist sea-salt spray through damp hair, concentrating on the mid-lengths rather than the roots, then scrunch upward in handfuls, squeezing each section toward the scalp for a few seconds.

Air-dry if you have time; if not, diffuse on low heat and low airflow, still scrunching as you go — our [diffuser guide for natural waves](/hairstyles/waves-curls/diffuser-natural-waves/) covers the technique in full. Once the hair is completely dry, break up any stiffness by scrunching again with dry hands. The honest limit of this method: on pin-straight hair, salt spray alone will not manufacture a wave. It adds grit and a slight bend at best, which is why straight hair belongs with the flat iron or wand methods above.

## Prep and Finishing: Texture Spray vs Hairspray

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/beach-waves/beach-waves-06.webp" alt="Finished beach waves being misted with texture spray at the roots, the waves staying matte and separated rather than glossy" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Whatever the method, prep is the same: start on clean, fully dry hair (or evenly damp hair for the heatless route), apply heat protectant before any hot tool, and keep oils and serums away from the lengths until after the waves are set — they add the gloss this style is trying to avoid.

Finishing is where texture spray and hairspray divide. Texture spray is the beach-wave product: it adds dry grit and separation, roughing the surface just enough that pieces stay distinct and the roots keep some lift. Use it last, in short bursts, and scrunch once more. Hairspray does a different job — it locks a shape in place — and used heavily it does two things this style hates: it adds shine and it glues the pieces back together. If your waves drop within the hour, a light mist of flexible-hold hairspray over the finished texture spray is a reasonable compromise. A full shell of strong-hold spray is a Hollywood finish applied to the wrong hairstyle.

## Beach Waves by Length

**Short hair (chin to jaw).** Use the flat iron or a smaller wand and take thinner sections — on short lengths, one wide section makes one wide bend and the style collapses into a flip. Leave proportionally more end out, and expect the result to read as a tousled texture rather than a wave pattern. Salt spray on naturally wavy short hair works well and takes two minutes.

**Medium hair (shoulder to collarbone).** The easiest length for this style: long enough for two or three bends per section, short enough to hold them. All four methods work. This is the length the classic beach wave was effectively designed around.

**Long hair.** Weight is the enemy — long waves drop from the bottom up. Work in smaller sections than feels necessary, let each piece cool in your palm rather than letting it hang while hot, and finish with texture spray through the mid-lengths, not just the roots. For the heatless route, add more braids; two braids on very long hair produce one bend, not a wave.

## The 5 Mistakes That Kill Beach Waves

**1. Curling all the way to the ends.** Bent, curled-under ends are the fastest tell of a styled curl. The straight last 2 cm are not a shortcut — they are the style. Leave them out of every tool, every time.

**2. Waving every section in the same direction.** Uniform direction merges separate waves into one large curl pattern, which is the mermaid and Hollywood look, not this one. Alternate relentlessly, including at the back where you cannot see what you are doing.

**3. Using too much product.** Beach waves collapse under weight and shine under gloss. One prep product, one finishing product, applied lightly, beats a five-step routine. If the hair feels coated, it will look coated.

**4. Brushing the waves out.** A brush merges the pieces and adds frizz in one pass. Separate with fingers, shake at the roots, and if a section looks too defined, pull it straight gently between your fingers rather than brushing it.

**5. Skipping heat protectant.** The flat iron and wand methods revisit the same sections repeatedly, and fine or lightened hair shows that damage as frizz — which then reads as more "beachy" for exactly one day before it reads as damaged. Protectant goes on before the first pass, not as an afterthought.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do beach waves last?

On most hair, heat-styled beach waves hold through the day and soften into a looser texture on day two, which many people prefer. Fine or very straight hair drops fastest and may need a quick flat-iron refresh at the front. Heatless braid waves typically last until the hair gets wet or is slept on for a second night.

### Can you get beach waves on straight hair?

Yes — straight hair is exactly what the flat iron and curling wand methods are for. The keys are alternating direction, leaving the ends straight, and finishing with texture spray for grip, because straight hair has no natural texture to hold the separation. Sea-salt spray alone will not create waves on pin-straight hair; it only enhances a pattern that already exists.

### What is the difference between beach waves and mermaid waves?

Beach waves are irregular, matte and separated, with straight undone ends — they imitate hair dried in sea air. Mermaid waves are a deep, uniform crimp made with a waver tool, where every ridge is the same depth and spacing. One is a texture, the other is a pattern; the full method is in our [mermaid waves guide](/hairstyles/waves-curls/mermaid-waves/).

### Do beach waves work on short hair?

Yes, with adjustments. On chin-to-jaw lengths, take thinner sections, use a flat iron or smaller wand barrel, and leave proportionally more of the ends out. The result reads as tousled texture rather than a repeating wave pattern, but it is the same style — and on naturally wavy short hair, a salt-spray scrunch gets there in minutes.

### Is sea salt spray bad for your hair?

Used occasionally, it is mostly a styling inconvenience rather than a damage problem: salt draws moisture out, so hair can feel dry and rough with daily use. Keep it to the mid-lengths, avoid it on already dry or heavily lightened ends, and wash it out rather than layering fresh spray over yesterday's. If your hair feels straw-like, switch to a salt-free texture spray, which gives similar grit without the drying effect.

### How do you refresh beach waves the next day?

Do not re-curl everything. Mist the lengths lightly with water or a texture spray, scrunch, and re-do only the front sections and any pieces that have fallen completely flat — usually three or four passes with the flat iron. Sleeping with the hair in one or two loose braids preserves the pattern better than a ponytail, which leaves a crease.
