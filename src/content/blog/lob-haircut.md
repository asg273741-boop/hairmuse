The lob — short for long bob — is the haircut that refuses to date. It sits at collarbone length, long enough to tie back and short enough to feel like a proper cut, and it works on almost every hair type because the shape is simple: a clean, mostly one-length perimeter with just enough internal shaping to stop it looking like a block.

That simplicity is also what makes the lob so adaptable. Keep it blunt and it reads polished and modern. Add soft layers and it moves like a longer cut. Cut it on an angle and it sharpens. Curl it and the same outline turns soft and romantic. Below are 12 lob styles worth taking to your stylist, plus an honest guide to how the lob differs from its two closest relatives.

## Lob vs Bob vs Medium-Length Layered: What Is the Difference?

These three cuts get confused constantly, and the difference matters because they behave differently day to day.

- **A bob** ends between the jaw and the shoulders. It is a true short cut: it rarely ties up, it needs trims every 6–8 weeks to hold its line, and it sits *above* the shoulders rather than on them. Our [bob haircuts guide](/hairstyles/bangs-bobs/bob-haircuts/) covers the bob family in full.
- **A lob** is the bob's longer sibling: collarbone length, give or take an inch. The defining feature is the perimeter — mostly one length, often blunt, with the structure of a bob rather than the flowing layering of a longer cut. It ties into a small ponytail, grows out gracefully, and can go 10–12 weeks between trims.
- **Medium-length layered hair** reaches a similar length but is built differently: the layers are the point, creating movement throughout rather than keeping a clean bob outline. If you want swing and softness over structure, our [medium length layered haircuts guide](/hairstyles/haircuts/medium-length-layered-haircuts/) is the sibling worth reading.

In short: choose a bob for a sharp short shape, a lob for bob structure with more length and flexibility, and medium layers for maximum movement.

## 1. Classic Blunt Lob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-01.webp" alt="Classic blunt lob haircut at collarbone length" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The reference lob: one length all the way around, ends cut straight across so the perimeter looks thick and deliberate. A centre or slightly off-centre part keeps it modern, and the ends should just graze the collarbone.

Blunt cutting is the kindest thing you can do for finer hair because nothing is thinned away. On thick hair, ask for the very ends to be softened underneath so the line sits flat rather than flaring.

## 2. Soft Layered Lob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-02.webp" alt="Soft layered lob with gentle movement" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Long, blended layers through the last few inches take the weight out of a lob without breaking up its outline. The layers should start below the chin — any higher and you are drifting into medium layered territory.

This is the version to choose if a blunt lob feels too severe on you, or if your hair is thick and a one-length cut sits like a triangle.

## 3. Wavy Lob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-03.webp" alt="Wavy lob with loose beachy bends" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Loose bends are the lob's most requested finish, and collarbone length is the sweet spot for them: long enough for the wave to form, short enough that it does not drag. Two or three large bends per section, ends left straight, gives the undone look that photographs so well.

Naturally wavy hair often only needs the ends refined and a little layering so the waves stack rather than puff. Straight hair holds this style best when the lob is cut a touch above the collarbone.

## 4. Curly Lob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-04.webp" alt="Curly lob with defined natural curls" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Curls shrink, so a curly lob is cut longer than it looks — what sits at the collarbone wet may spring to the shoulders dry. Ask for it cut dry, curl by curl, with gentle layering to stop the shape widening at the bottom.

Keep the perimeter soft rather than blunt. A hard line fights curl pattern; a slightly rounded edge lets each curl sit in its own space.

## 5. Lob with Curtain Bangs

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-05.webp" alt="Lob with curtain bangs and face-framing layers" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Curtain bangs are the easiest fringe to pair with a lob because they melt into the front instead of sitting on top of it. The shortest point should skim the cheekbones, then blend into light face framing.

This combination flatters almost every face shape and grows out with no awkward stage. If you are fringe-curious but commitment-shy, start here — and see our [curtain bangs guide](/hairstyles/bangs-bobs/curtain-bangs-guide/) for styling and grow-out advice.

## 6. Angled A-Line Lob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-06.webp" alt="Angled A-line lob, longer at the front" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

An A-line lob is shorter at the back and gradually longer toward the front, so the front points frame the jaw. The angle can be subtle — an inch of difference — or dramatic.

It is the sharpest-looking lob and the least forgiving: the angle only reads if the hair is worn smooth and the ends are trimmed on schedule. Wavy and curly hair blur the line, so this version suits straight to slightly wavy textures best.

## 7. Asymmetrical Lob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-07.webp" alt="Asymmetrical lob with one side longer" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

One side is cut noticeably longer than the other, usually paired with a deep side part so the longer side sweeps across. It is a deliberate, editorial take on the lob that still keeps collarbone length on the long side.

The trade-off is styling flexibility: the asymmetry is built for one parting, and flipping your part will undo the effect. Choose it if you wear your hair the same way most days.

## 8. Textured Lob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-08.webp" alt="Textured lob with choppy piecey ends" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Point-cut, piecey ends and a matte, tousled finish give the lob a rockier edge. The texture is cut into the last inch or two, so the outline stays lob-shaped while the ends break up.

This version loves second-day hair and a pinch of texturising product worked through the ends. Fine hair should ask for a light hand — too much point-cutting and the ends turn wispy instead of choppy.

## 9. Lob for Fine Hair

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-09.webp" alt="Lob for fine hair with a full blunt perimeter" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Collarbone length is arguably the best length fine hair can wear: long enough to feel like a style, short enough that the ends still look full. The rules are blunt perimeter, minimal layering, and no thinning shears anywhere near the ends.

A slight inward bend from a round brush, or one loose wave through the mid-lengths, adds the appearance of body without removing any actual hair.

## 10. Lob for Thick Hair

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-10.webp" alt="Lob for thick hair with internal layers" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Thick hair in a one-length lob can balloon at the bottom. The fix is invisible: internal layers and undercutting beneath the surface remove bulk while the top layer keeps the clean lob line.

Ask your stylist to debulk from the inside rather than layering the surface, and keep the length at or just below the collarbone so the weight that remains works for the shape instead of against it.

## 11. Lob Over 50

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-11.webp" alt="Soft lob on a woman over 50 with silver hair" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The lob is a genuinely flattering length over 50: it lifts the eye to the collarbone, keeps enough hair for softness around the face, and avoids both the severity of a short bob and the drag of long hair.

Soft layers, a side part and face framing tend to flatter more than a blunt centre-parted version. Silver and grey hair look particularly striking in this cut because the clean perimeter shows off the colour's natural dimension.

## 12. Low-Maintenance Grow-Out Lob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/lob-haircut/lob-haircut-12.webp" alt="Low-maintenance grown-out lob with soft ends" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

If you want the lob without the appointment diary, ask for it cut slightly below the collarbone with soft, lightly layered ends and no fringe. There is no sharp line to lose, so the shape simply relaxes into medium-length hair over three to four months.

This is also the kindest route if you are growing out a bob: one reshaping appointment turns the bob's hard line into this softer lob, and from there it grows out on its own.

## How to Style a Lob

The lob's everyday styling is simple. For the classic finish, blow-dry smooth and bend the ends very slightly under or away from the face — a completely straight, flat-ironed lob can look dated, while a small bend keeps it current. For waves, work in large sections and leave the ends out of the tong. Air-dried, a lob benefits from being tucked behind the ears while it dries to encourage a soft face-framing curve. Whatever the finish, the perimeter is the style: keep the ends trimmed and conditioned and the cut does most of the work itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What length is a lob haircut?

A lob sits at or just below the collarbone, typically around 30–35 cm of length from the crown depending on height and neck length. If it ends above the shoulders it is a bob; well past the collarbone and it becomes medium-length hair.

### Does a lob suit every face shape?

Nearly. The collarbone length and vertical line flatter round, square and heart-shaped faces, and the angle or asymmetry can be adjusted for longer faces. A side part softens strong jawlines, while a centre part suits balanced, oval proportions.

### Is a lob good for fine hair?

Yes — it is one of the best cuts for fine hair. The blunt, mostly one-length perimeter keeps the ends looking as full as possible. Ask for minimal layering and no thinning shears through the ends.

### How often does a lob need trimming?

A blunt or angled lob holds its line best with a trim every 8–10 weeks. Softer, layered and grow-out versions can stretch to 12 weeks or more because there is no precise edge to lose.

### Can you tie a lob up?

Usually, yes — that is one of its advantages over a bob. Most collarbone-length lobs pull into a low ponytail or small bun, though the shortest back layers of an angled lob may escape and need pinning.

### What is the difference between a lob and a layered medium haircut?

The lob keeps a bob's clean, mostly one-length outline at collarbone length. A medium layered haircut uses layers throughout as the main source of shape and movement, so its perimeter looks softer and less structured.

## Final Thoughts

The lob endures because it asks very little and gives a lot back: a proper haircut shape, enough length to vary and tie up, and a grow-out that does not punish you for missing an appointment. Start blunt if your hair is fine, layered if it is thick, angled if you wear it smooth, and soft-ended if you want the lowest maintenance.
