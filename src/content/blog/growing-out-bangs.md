The awkward stage of growing out bangs is usually about placement, not length alone. The front pieces are long enough to fall into your eyes but too short to stay behind your ears. A change of part or one well-placed accessory can keep them comfortable without cutting them again.

Hair grows roughly 1.25 cm (about half an inch) a month on average, so growing a fringe out fully is the better part of a year — but most of the frustration sits in the first few months, and each stage along the way has a style that suits it.

Work with your natural growth pattern where possible. You may need a small amount of water, a comb, a light styling product and a couple of clips.

## The grow-out, stage by stage

**Stage one: in your eyes (months 1–3).** The fringe has dropped past your brows and brushes your lashes, but is too short to move aside and stay there. This stage tempts people back to the scissors; side sweeps, clips and headbands do the heavy lifting.

**Stage two: the nose-and-cheek stage (months 3–6).** The ends reach between your nose and cheekbones. A centre part starts to work: each side curves away from the face in a soft curtain shape, even if it springs loose by midday. Twists and small hairline braids come into their own here too.

**Stage three: nearly there (months 6–9).** The front pieces tuck behind your ears, sometimes with a pin for security, and sit happily in ponytails and updos. Now ask a stylist to blend the ends into your face-framing layers, so the last line of the old fringe disappears.

**Stage four: grown out (months 9–12).** The former fringe reads as face-framing layers. You can still part it in the middle for a curtain effect or sweep it aside, but it no longer needs managing — it is simply part of the haircut.

## 1. The side-swept clip

Make a slightly deeper side part than usual. Comb the fringe across the forehead and secure it just above the opposite temple with a slim barrette. If the ends stick out, mist them lightly and smooth them under the longer side hair before clipping. This works especially well when the fringe reaches the outer corner of your eye.

Train the direction while the hair is damp: a fringe blow-dried straight down for months will try to fall forward, but two or three weeks of drying it to the side while wet retrains the roots. Keep a spare clip in your bag for windy days.

## 2. A soft middle-part sweep

Wet only the front section. Split it roughly in the middle, then direct each side away from the face while drying on low heat. A small round brush can give the ends a gentle bend; your fingers work if you want a looser shape. Let the hair cool in place before touching it. This encourages a curtain-like shape without requiring a new cut.

If this is the look you are growing towards, read up on [how curtain bangs are cut and styled](/hairstyles/bangs-bobs/curtain-bangs-guide/) — the same drying technique applies, and at cheekbone length a stylist can snip the centre pieces a touch shorter so the sweep opens properly instead of hanging as two straight curtains.

## 3. Two small twists

Part the front section where it naturally wants to separate. Take a narrow piece on one side, twist it away from the face and add a little hair from just behind the fringe. Secure the twist with a bobby pin above the ear. Repeat on the other side. Including a little longer hair gives each pin more to hold than the fringe alone.

At cheekbone length, a small braid along the hairline works the same way and holds even better — a three-strand plait that picks up hair as it goes absorbs the fringe completely. New to braiding your own hairline? This guide to the [different types of braids](/hairstyles/braids/types-of-braids/) covers the basics, and a Dutch-style plait hides the join where fringe meets longer hair.

## 4. A comfortable headband

Place a soft band a short distance behind the hairline rather than pushing every short piece straight back. Pull a few longer face-framing strands forward if you like a less severe shape. If the band slides, try one with a fabric or comb grip instead of tightening it against your scalp.

Slicked-back days are rescue styling too: work a pea-sized amount of gel through damp front pieces, comb everything back and let it set, or gather the lot into an updo so the fringe disappears into it. A [claw clip French twist](/hairstyles/updos-buns/claw-clip-french-twist/) is ideal, because the twist swallows shorter pieces that would escape a ponytail. Keep fully-swept styles for occasional days — constant tension at the hairline stresses the strands you are growing.

## Keep the shape manageable

Use a little product rather than a heavy layer. Too much wax can make growing-out pieces separate into obvious strands. If the fringe keeps falling into your eyes, change the part or pin placement first. When you are ready for a trim, tell your stylist that you are growing it out; a small shape adjustment can blend the front into the rest of the haircut without taking you back to short bangs.

## Trim without restarting the clock

Growing out does not mean never cutting. Ragged ends make the stage look scruffier than it is. The answer is the micro-trim: every eight to twelve weeks, have the last few millimetres "dusted" off the front pieces. At 1.25 cm of growth a month, a dusting removes only a fraction of your gains, so length still moves forward while the ends stay tidy.

When you book, say it plainly: "I'm growing my fringe out — please don't shorten it, just clean up the ends and soften the corners." The corners matter most: rounding the outer edges turns a blunt line into a slope that sweeps and parts easily. If a stylist offers to "tidy it back to brow length," that is a reset, not a trim.

Colour can disguise the stage, too: a solid block of colour reads as a deliberate fringe for longer, while soft face-framing highlights make the growing pieces look like intentional layers instead.

The mistakes that restart the clock are usually impatience in disguise: trimming the fringe yourself "just a centimetre" (which becomes three), or booking a full restyle at the three-month peak. Heat damage is the hidden setback — daily straightening can snap the front ends, erasing months of growth.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take to grow out bangs completely?

Around nine to twelve months for a brow-length fringe to blend into jaw-length layers, at average growth of roughly 1.25 cm a month. It feels manageable much sooner: by months three to four it sweeps or parts reliably, and by six months it tucks behind your ears. Your timeline shifts with growth rate and starting length, so treat the stages above as a rough map, not a deadline.

### Should I trim my hair while growing out bangs?

Yes — but trim the ends, not the length. A micro-trim or "dusting" every eight to twelve weeks removes the last few millimetres, tidying split ends while keeping almost all your growth. Skipping trims backfires: frayed ends look unkempt and split further up the strand. The front pieces should never return to brow length.

### How do I style bangs that keep falling into my eyes?

Work on damp hair, because direction is set as it dries. Blow-dry the fringe firmly to one side, or split it and dry each half away from your face, then hold it with a clip while it cools. A little light mousse at the roots adds grip without heavy wax's stringiness. If it still escapes, add a physical anchor — a barrette, pinned twist or headband — rather than more product.

### How can I hide growing-out bangs for a formal event?

Take the front pieces out of play: sweep everything back into a smooth updo, French twist or low bun, with gel on damp hair and crossed pins over the fringe section. A small hairline braid is the softer alternative — it absorbs the fringe and reads as deliberate rather than hidden. Avoid relying on hairspray alone over a down style; growing-out pieces work themselves free during an evening.

### Can I convert growing-out bangs into curtain bangs instead?

Yes — it is one of the easiest exits from the awkward stage, because curtain bangs are meant to be long. Once the centre pieces reach between your nose and cheekbones, a stylist can cut a shorter centre with longer sides so the fringe parts and drapes instead of hanging straight. From there you can keep the curtain shape or keep growing; angled sides blend into layers more gracefully than a blunt fringe. Ask explicitly — it is a shaping cut, not a shortening one.

### Which stage of growing out bangs looks the worst — and how do I get through it?

Most people find the first stage, months one to three, the hardest: the fringe is in your eyes constantly but too short to tuck, pin neatly or part. It is also the shortest stage, and where accessories do the most work. The awkwardness peaks just before the length tips into sweepable, partable territory — so the week you most want to cut it is usually the last week you need to.
