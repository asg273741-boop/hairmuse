A straightener looks like a one-job tool, but it is quietly the most versatile styler most people own. With the same pair of plates you can make three distinct curl families: polished ribbon curls, loose undone waves, and soft S-shaped bends — no curling iron, no second appliance. If you can straighten your hair, you already own the tool; what you are learning here is the hand movement.

The reason it works is simple once you see it. Hair takes the shape it is held in while it heats and cools. A curling iron wraps hair around a hot barrel; a straightener instead clamps the hair flat and twists as it glides, so the plates press a spiral into the strand as it passes through them. Control the twist and the glide speed, and you control the curl.

This guide covers the core technique first, then the three variations, adjustments for short and long hair, the mistakes behind kinks and dropped curls, and how to make the result last — plus an honest section on heat.

## What You Need

- **A straightener with plates around 2.5 cm wide** — plate width matters more than brand. Narrow plates (roughly 2–2.5 cm) can turn inside a curl and suit short to medium hair; 2.5 cm is the sweet spot for most lengths. Wide plates (4 cm and up) barely rotate — they make bends at best, not curls. Rounded plate edges help too: hair slides over them without creasing.
- **Heat protectant spray** — non-negotiable with a hot tool.
- **Sectioning clips** — four to six; you will work in layers.
- **A heat-resistant glove** — optional, but it makes the learning stage less fiddly — plus a comb for parting and sectioning.
- **Finishing products** — a light hairspray or texture spray, plus a drop of hair oil for the ends if you use it.

## Prep: Dry Hair, Protection, Sections

**Start on fully dry hair.** This is the one rule with no exceptions. Hair must be completely dry — not towel-damp, not "mostly dry" — before the plates touch it. Curling damp hair steams the water inside the strand, which is both damaging and pointless: the curl will not set and will drop within the hour.

**Apply heat protectant to dry hair** and comb it through so every section is coated, then let it dry down for a minute.

**Section before you heat.** Divide the hair into bottom, middle and top layers, clipping the upper layers out of the way. Work one layer at a time, taking vertical sections roughly 2–3 cm wide — about the width of the plates. Oversized sections are the most common beginner mistake: the outside curls while the inside barely warms.

## Heat Settings by Hair Type

There is no single correct temperature — there is a correct range for your hair, and the honest version is to use the lowest heat that actually sets a curl in it. Test on one section at the back: if the curl springs up and holds as it cools, the heat is enough.

- **Fine, fragile or colour-treated hair: 150–170°C.** Fine hair shapes easily and damages easily; it rarely needs more.
- **Medium, normal hair: 170–190°C.** The working range for most people — enough to set a lasting curl in one pass.
- **Thick, coarse or very resistant hair: 190–210°C.** Coarse hair needs the higher end to change shape, but repeated passes at top heat are where damage accumulates — one slow, steady pass beats three fast ones.

And never compensate for the wrong temperature by going over the same section repeatedly — if a curl does not take, let it cool and check your technique first; speed and section size are usually the real problem.

## The Core Technique, Step by Step

Every straightener curl is built on one movement: **clamp, twist, glide**. Learn it on the bottom layer where mistakes do not show; the motion feels awkward for ten minutes and automatic after that.

### Step 1: Clamp Near the Root

Take your first section and clamp the straightener horizontally near the root — close, but not hard against the scalp; leave a centimetre or two. Keep the clamp gentle: squeezing too hard prints sharp clamp marks.

### Step 2: Twist the Iron 180° Away from the Face

Rotate the straightener half a turn — 180° — so the plates turn away from your face and the section wraps over the back of the iron. The hair should now be looped around the plates, pointing back the way it came. This twist is the curl; everything after is just feeding the rest of the strand through the same shape.

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/how-to-curl-hair-with-a-straightener/how-to-curl-hair-with-a-straightener-02.webp" alt="Twisting a straightener 180 degrees away from the face to begin a curl" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

### Step 3: Glide Slowly and Steadily to the Ends

Keeping the twist held, glide the iron down the section in one slow, continuous movement — roughly two to three seconds for a shoulder-length section. Slow and steady is the whole secret: hair needs time between the plates to take the shape, and a constant speed means an even curl from root to tip. Do not stop mid-section — that pause prints a kink.

### Step 4: Release and Let It Cool Untouched

Open the plates at the ends and let the curl fall into your palm, spring-shaped. Leave it exactly as it is — do not pull it out, rake it or brush it while warm. A curl sets as it cools, and disturbing it hot undoes the work. Move straight to the next section; do all separating and styling at the end, when every curl is cold.

### Step 5: Work Up Through the Layers, Curling Away from the Face

Release the next layer and repeat. On both sides, keep the twist direction consistent: **curl away from the face**, especially for the front sections. Front pieces curled away open the face up and look deliberate; pieces curled towards it close it in and tangle. Alternating direction through the back layers adds natural volume, but the front sections should always go away from the face.

## Variation 1: Ribbon Curls

Ribbon curls are the polished, defined member of the family — smooth spirals like lengths of ribbon. They are the core technique with everything turned up slightly.

Twist a **full 360°** instead of 180° — one complete turn of the iron — and glide **more slowly** than feels natural, keeping the section taut as it feeds through. The extra rotation wraps the hair further around the plates and the slower pass gives it more time in the heat, producing a tighter, springier, more uniform curl. Take slightly smaller sections for maximum definition. Ribbon curls suit occasions and photos, and hair that drops curl quickly — they relax over the day into soft waves rather than disappearing.

## Variation 2: Loose Waves

Loose waves are the everyday version — relaxed and undone. They use the standard **180° twist** with two changes: glide **faster**, and **leave the last 3–5 cm of the ends out** of the twist entirely.

As you approach the ends, open the plates slightly and pull the iron straight down off the hair rather than twisting through the tips. Ends left straight are what separate a modern wave from a formal curl — the wave lives in the mid-lengths. Take larger sections than for ribbon curls, and once everything is cool, rake through with fingers or a wide-tooth comb to break the waves up.

## Variation 3: The Flat-Iron Wave (S-Bend)

The S-bend makes no spiral at all. Instead of twisting, you **rock the iron** as you feed the hair through it, bending the strand alternately one way then the other so it sets into a flat S pattern — an editorial wave that lies flatter than either curl type.

Clamp the section near the root and push the hair into a C shape with your free hand as the iron holds it; glide down a few centimetres, then bend the hair the opposite way into a reverse C as you continue. Keep alternating — C, reverse C, C — all the way down, moving slowly so each bend takes the heat. There is **no twist** in this variation; the shape comes purely from the rocking, bending motion. It works best on medium to long hair with length for at least two full bends.

## Short Hair and Long Hair Adjustments

**Short hair (bob length and above):** use the narrowest plates you have — around 2–2.5 cm — and take smaller sections, because there is less hair to feed through the twist. A half-twist often beats a full one: short sections over-rotate easily into tight ringlets or flicks. Keep the glide short and controlled, and expect waves and bends to be your most reliable styles — a ribbon curl needs length to wrap.

**Long hair:** sectioning stops being optional. Work in three or four layers, keep sections at plate width so the whole strand heats evenly, and resist speeding up as you tire — the top layers are the ones everyone sees. Pin each curl to cool: long hair's own weight pulls curls straighter as they set.

## Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

**Clamp kinks — those sharp little dents across the curl.** Caused by pausing mid-glide, clamping too hard, or starting the twist after the iron has sat still on the hair. The fix is one continuous motion: clamp, twist immediately, keep moving. If a kink happens, let the section cool before re-doing it.

**Curls that drop within the hour.** Usually the glide was too fast for the heat to register, the sections too big for the plates to heat through, or the curls brushed while still warm. Slow down, halve the section size, and keep your hands off until everything is cold.

**Sections too big.** If a section is wider than the plates or so thick the iron struggles to close, its middle never really curls. Take less hair than you think you need.

**Curling damp hair.** The most damaging mistake on this list. Sizzling is the sound of water boiling inside the strand. Always start bone dry — including letting heat protectant dry down after spraying.

## Making Curls Last: Cool, Pin, Then Product

A curl is only half-formed when it leaves the iron — it finishes forming as it cools. Three habits decide whether curls last an evening or until lunch.

**Let every curl cool completely before touching it.** The highest-value habit in this guide: warm hair is still pliable, cold hair has set. Do the whole head first, then style.

**Pin curls while they cool, for maximum hold.** As each curl comes off the iron, wind it back into its spiral with your fingers, pin it flat against the head, and leave the pins in until the whole head is cool. The curl sets in its tightest form instead of sagging under its own weight while warm — the difference-maker for fine or long hair and humid days.

**Get the product order right.** Heat protectant goes on before the iron, always — it is the only product that belongs under heat. Everything else comes after: separate the cooled curls with your fingers, add a drop of oil to dry-looking ends, then finish with a light mist of hairspray or texture spray at arm's length.

## An Honest Note on Heat

Straightener curls are quick, versatile and genuinely flattering — and they are still heat styling. Every pass of a hot plate costs the hair some moisture and, over time, some strength, particularly at higher temperatures and on fine, bleached or already-damaged hair. Heat protectant reduces that cost; it does not eliminate it. Use the lowest heat that sets your curl, keep hot-tool curling to a few times a week rather than daily, and never re-curl dry, brittle-feeling hair without a wash and condition in between.

And some days the right answer is no heat at all: [sock curls](/hairstyles/waves-curls/sock-curls/) set soft, bouncy curls overnight with two socks and damp hair, and our guide to [heatless curls overnight](/hairstyles/waves-curls/heatless-curls-overnight/) compares the sock, ribbon-curler and topknot methods side by side. Alternating heat days with heatless days keeps both the curls and the hair.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why won't my hair hold a straightener curl?

Usually technique, not hair: gliding too fast, sections too big for the plates, or touching the curls while still warm. Slow the glide to two to three seconds per section, halve your section size, and leave every curl untouched until completely cold. If curls still drop, pin each one to cool — setting in the spiral under no weight fixes most stubborn cases.

### Which way should I twist the straightener?

Away from your face, on both sides, for the sections that frame it — this opens the face up and stops the front pieces tangling into each other. Through the back layers you can alternate direction for a more natural result, but keep the front sections going away from the face, and hold the same twist for the whole glide.

### Can I curl short hair with a straightener?

Yes — waves and bends more reliably than full curls. Use narrow plates (around 2–2.5 cm), take smaller sections, and try a half-twist rather than a full rotation, since short sections over-rotate into tight flicks. The loose wave and S-bend suit bob-length hair best; above jaw length, expect a bend at the ends rather than a true curl.

### What temperature should I use to curl my hair?

Match it to your hair type: 150–170°C for fine, fragile or colour-treated hair; 170–190°C for medium, normal hair; 190–210°C for thick, coarse or resistant hair. Start at the bottom of your range and test one section at the back — if the curl springs up and holds as it cools, the heat is sufficient. The lowest temperature that sets a curl is the right one.

### How do I stop clamp marks and kinks?

Keep the iron moving. Kinks come from pausing mid-section, clamping down hard, or letting the iron sit still before the twist. Use a gentle clamp, twist immediately, and glide in one continuous motion to the ends. If a kink appears, let the section cool fully before re-doing it.

### Are straightener curls more damaging than a curling iron?

Neither is gentle — both apply hot metal to hair — but a straightener pass adds clamping pressure to the heat, and beginners often make several passes over the same section while learning. Once your technique is consistent, one steady pass per section is comparable to a curling iron. The rules are the same either way: fully dry hair, heat protectant, the lowest effective temperature, and heatless methods on the days in between.

## The Short Version

One tool, one movement: clamp near the root, twist away from the face, glide slowly, and let the curl cool untouched. Turn the twist up to 360° for ribbon curls, speed up and skip the ends for loose waves, or drop the twist and rock the iron for S-bends. Keep sections small, hair bone dry, heat matched to your hair type, and products in the right order.
