A crown braid is a plait that travels all the way around the head, following the hairline like a headband or sitting higher like a circlet, with the ends tucked and pinned out of sight. It is one of the oldest updos there is — part Greek statue, part milkmaid, part modern wedding guest — and it earns its keep: every strand is off the face and neck, nothing swings or slips, and the style regularly survives a full day and a night's sleep. The twelve versions below run from the classic full circuit to softer half crowns and occasion builds, followed by a full step-by-step for braiding the classic version on your own head.

Two foundations carry almost everything here. The classic crown is built as a Dutch braid — if the under-crossing movement is new to you, our [Dutch braid tutorial](/hairstyles/braids/how-to-dutch-braid/) teaches the hand positions first. The half crown leans on French braid technique instead, covered in our [French braid tutorial](/hairstyles/braids/how-to-french-braid/).

## 1. Classic Full Crown Braid

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-01.webp" alt="Woman with chestnut brown hair in a classic full crown braid wrapped neatly around her head" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The reference version: one continuous Dutch braid that starts behind one ear, travels around the back, across the front hairline, and returns to its starting point, where the tail is tucked underneath and pinned flat. Every strand is up, the silhouette is a clean ring, and the braid itself becomes the accessory. This is the style the step-by-step later in this article teaches. **Difficulty:** hard the first time — braiding behind your own head is a direction problem, not a skill problem — moderate once your hands know the route. **Length:** shoulder length at the very least; armpit length gives the tail enough reach to tuck.

## 2. Half Crown Braid

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-02.webp" alt="Woman with shoulder-length blonde hair wearing a half crown braid across the top of her head with the rest of her hair worn loose" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Only the top section makes the journey: a French or Dutch braid worked from one side of the head to the other across the crown, while everything below is left loose. It reads as a plaited headband and is the most forgiving crown here — the route is short, all of it visible, and mistakes are easy to reach. It is also the short-hair option: a long bob manages it comfortably, because the braid only crosses the head rather than circling it. **Difficulty:** easy to moderate. **Length:** chin length and up.

## 3. Crown Braid with Loose Waves

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-03.webp" alt="Woman with long honey brown hair in a crown braid with soft loose waves falling over her shoulders" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

A full crown braid worn as the anchor rather than the whole style: the braid completes its circuit, but the lengths below are left down in soft waves, so the crown sits like a plaited band above flowing hair. In practice most people build this as a deep half crown with extra hair fed in, which suits long but fine hair — a true full crown on fine hair can look sparse. Wave the loose hair first, braid second. A [waterfall braid](/hairstyles/braids/how-to-waterfall-braid/) achieves a similar softness if the crown route defeats you. **Difficulty:** moderate. **Length:** armpit length or longer.

## 4. Double Crown Braid

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-04.webp" alt="Woman with dark brown hair wearing two parallel crown braids circling her head" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Two thinner braids circling the head in parallel instead of one thick one — the first follows the hairline, the second runs a few centimetres inside it, and the pair reads as a plaited coronet. Braid the outer ring first and pin it, then work the inner ring; the second braid is far harder to start once the first is in the way. A slightly uneven stitch also disappears when two rows compete for attention. **Difficulty:** hard — it is the classic crown, twice. **Length:** armpit length or longer, with enough body that two thin braids still show.

## 5. Crown Braid into a Low Bun

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-05.webp" alt="Woman with ash blonde hair in a crown braid that flows into a low bun at the nape of her neck" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The braid makes most of its circuit, then instead of tucking the tail under, everything gathers at the nape and twists into a low bun, with the braid feeding into it. This solves the classic crown's one awkward moment — the bulky tucked tail — by giving the length somewhere better to go, and the bun makes the style read formal. It is the version most often chosen for weddings and black-tie events, usually with the bun slightly off-centre. **Difficulty:** moderate to hard. **Length:** shoulder length or longer; the bun needs a little spare length to form.

## 6. Milkmaid Crown Braid

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-06.webp" alt="Woman with golden blonde hair in a milkmaid crown braid with two plaits crossed over the top of her head" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Honesty first: this is not a cousin of the milkmaid braid, it *is* the milkmaid braid, listed here because most people searching for crown braids are picturing exactly this. The construction is completely different — no braiding around the head at all. The hair is parted down the middle, plaited into two ordinary braids hanging by the ears, then each braid is lifted up and over the top of the head and pinned on the opposite side, ends tucked under. Dramatically easier than a true crown, with no blind braiding. **Difficulty:** easy. **Length:** armpit length or longer — each plait must reach across the head and pin.

## 7. Dutch Crown Braid

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-07.webp" alt="Woman with dark hair in a raised Dutch crown braid standing out boldly from her head" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The classic crown's bolder sibling. The route is identical — one continuous circuit — but every section is crossed firmly under, the braid kept deliberately raised and tight, and afterwards each loop is pulled outwards slightly so the ridge stands as tall as possible. Where the classic aims for smooth and seamless, this version wants the braid seen from across the room. It also holds the best of any crown here, because the raised stitches lock against each other. **Difficulty:** hard. **Length:** shoulder length or longer; thick hair shows the raised pattern best.

## 8. Fishtail Crown Braid

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-08.webp" alt="Woman with strawberry blonde hair in a fishtail crown braid with a fine herringbone pattern around her head" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The fine herringbone pattern of a fishtail, worked around the head in place of a three-strand braid: a lace fishtail, with small pieces crossed from the outside edges and new hair fed in from the hairline side only, so the braid hugs the head as it travels. It is slow — roughly twice as many crossings as a plait — but the finished texture is the most intricate on this list, and the small sections make even a slim braid look detailed. **Difficulty:** hard, and the most time-consuming here. **Length:** armpit length or longer.

## 9. Boho Pancaked Crown Braid

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-09.webp" alt="Woman with wavy light brown hair in a loose pancaked crown braid with soft strands framing her face" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The undone crown: braid the classic circuit loosely, then go back around and pull every loop gently outward — pancaking — until the braid looks twice its real width, soft and slightly dishevelled, with a few strands freed around the face. Start with waved or second-day hair, never freshly smoothed lengths; the texture keeps the loosened braid from falling apart. It is the most forgiving crown for beginners: pancaking hides uneven tension completely. **Difficulty:** moderate. **Length:** shoulder length or longer; layers help the softness.

## 10. Crown Braid with Fresh Flowers

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-10.webp" alt="Woman with dark blonde hair in a crown braid decorated with small white fresh flowers tucked along the braid" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Any crown on this list — most often the classic or the boho version — with small fresh flowers tucked into the braid's loops after it is finished and pinned. Gypsophila, small roses and waxflower survive best; push the stems in at an angle so the plait itself grips them, and pin any stem that feels loose. Build the braid first, decorate last. It is the definitive summer wedding-guest and festival crown. **Difficulty:** the base braid's difficulty, plus ten patient minutes. **Length:** whatever the base style needs.

## 11. Side-Swept Crown Braid

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-11.webp" alt="Woman with auburn hair in a side-swept crown braid beginning at a deep side parting" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

A deep side parting changes the whole geometry: the braid starts above one ear, sweeps across the front hairline on a diagonal rather than following it straight, circles the back, and finishes tucked behind the opposite ear. The asymmetry is the point — a sweeping, almost vintage front line that a centred crown cannot give, and more flattering on round and square faces. Mark the parting crisply before you start; a soft part will wander as you braid. **Difficulty:** hard. **Length:** shoulder length or longer.

## 12. Bridal Crown Braid Updo

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/crown-braid-hairstyles/crown-braid-hairstyles-12.webp" alt="Woman with elegant bridal crown braid updo with all her hair pinned up and a few delicate pearl pins" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The full occasion build: a classic or Dutch crown with every remaining strand pinned up inside the ring, so nothing hangs down at all, finished with pearl pins, a fine comb or a veil anchored into the braid itself — one of the few updos a veil grips securely. Salons build it over curled, backcombed hair for volume, with far more pins than a daytime version. Practise the whole build at least once before the day. **Difficulty:** hard, usually stylist-built for a wedding. **Length:** shoulder length or longer.

## How to Braid a Classic Crown Braid, Step by Step

This is style 1, broken down. Allow twenty minutes the first time and do not schedule it before an event — the second attempt is reliably twice as fast as the first.

**Step 1 — Prepare the hair.** Start with dry, second-day hair, or work dry shampoo or texture spray through freshly washed hair. Brush out every tangle, then part the hair where you normally wear it. Slip a thin elastic over your wrist and have bobby pins within reach — mid-braid is the wrong moment to go hunting for them.

**Step 2 — Section and start behind one ear.** Begin behind your right ear (mirror this if you are left-handed). Take a section about three fingers wide at the hairline and split it into three strands. The braid starts here; the tail will eventually come back around and tuck underneath this exact spot.

**Step 3 — Dutch braid up and over.** Cross the side strands *under* the middle strand — the Dutch movement from our [Dutch braid tutorial](/hairstyles/braids/how-to-dutch-braid/) — adding a small piece of new hair to each side strand as you go. Work up past the ear and across the front hairline, feeding in hair from both sides so the braid travels in a ring rather than spiralling inwards. Keep your hands close to the head; the angle feels strange and that is normal.

**Step 4 — Around the back.** As the braid turns across the back of your head, your hands swap roles and you will be braiding by feel. Slow down rather than tightening your grip — tension spikes are what make the back section bulge. Keep adding hair in small, even pieces. If your arms tire, rest them while pinching the braid firmly with one hand; a held braid waits, a released one unravels.

**Step 5 — Finish the circuit.** When you return to your starting point, you will run out of hair to add. Continue as a plain three-strand plait down the remaining length and secure the end with the small elastic from your wrist.

**Step 6 — Tuck and pin.** Lay the loose plait flat along the crown braid's line, sliding it underneath the starting section, and pin every few centimetres, pushing pins in against the braid's direction so they lock rather than slide. The join should vanish under the first stitches. Cross two pins in an X at the join — that point carries the strain.

**Step 7 — Pancake and soften.** Go around the ring tugging each outer loop gently outward, a few millimetres at a time, so the widening stays even. This — pancaking — turns a tight, thin circuit into the full, soft crown in the photographs. Finish with a light mist of hairspray and smooth flyaways down with your palms rather than more product.

## A Note on Length: The Honest Version

Crown braids are not a short-hair style, and no amount of pinning changes the geometry. A full circuit needs roughly shoulder-length hair at minimum — the plait has to travel all the way around your head and still have a tail left to tuck — and armpit length is where it becomes comfortable rather than barely possible. Chin-length and bob wearers have one genuine option here: the half crown (style 2), which only crosses the top of the head. Below chin length, wear a braid across the front hairline instead — a similar face-framing line for a fraction of the length.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a crown braid the same as a milkmaid braid?

No — they look alike in photographs but are built differently. A crown braid is one continuous braid woven around the head. A milkmaid braid is two ordinary plaits made hanging down, then lifted up, crossed over the top of the head and pinned. The milkmaid is far easier, and appears on this list as style 6, clearly labelled.

### How long does a crown braid take to do?

On someone else, ten to fifteen minutes once practised. On your own head, allow twenty to thirty minutes for the first few attempts — the braiding is not slow, but working behind your own head takes concentration. A half crown or the milkmaid version takes five to ten minutes, as neither involves blind braiding.

### How long does hair need to be for a crown braid?

Roughly shoulder length for a full crown, armpit length for a comfortable one with a tail that tucks away neatly. The half crown works from chin length. Much shorter than shoulder length, the braid simply runs out of hair partway around the back — pins cannot substitute for the missing length.

### Can a crown braid be slept in?

Yes, and it is one of its genuine advantages. A classic or Dutch crown braided snugly usually survives the night and looks softer, not worse, the next day. Sleep on a smooth pillowcase; in the morning, smooth the surface with damp palms and re-pin any loose loop. Two days is the realistic limit.

### Does a crown braid work on fine or thin hair?

It does, with adjustments. Fine hair makes a slimmer circuit, so pancake generously, add texture spray beforehand for grip and apparent bulk, and consider the double crown — two thinner braids disguise sparseness better than one. Avoid the fishtail crown on very fine hair; the small sections can make it look thinner still.

### Is a crown braid suitable for a wedding?

It is one of the classic bridal updos: formal-looking, off the face for photographs, stable for the full day without touch-ups, and a veil or comb gets something solid to anchor into. Build it over curled hair for volume, pin more heavily than a daytime version, and always do one full practice run in advance.

## Final Thoughts

A crown braid rewards exactly one thing: a practice run. The technique is a Dutch braid you probably already know, bent into a circle — the only new skill is direction, and direction becomes automatic by the third attempt. Start with the milkmaid if you want the look today, graduate to the half crown, and take on the full circuit when you have a quiet twenty minutes.
