An A-line bob is a bob cut shorter at the back and longer at the front, so the bottom edge runs on one clean forward angle — high at the nape, low at the chin, like the slant of the letter A. An A-line bob is defined by its length line only: where the hair ends, and the angle those ends make.

It says nothing about layers. A true A-line perimeter is cut in one length, which is exactly what separates it from its closest relative, the stacked bob — a distinction the comparison section below settles properly. What the angle gives you is movement without layers: the front points swing, frame the jaw and pull the eye forward and down, while the shorter back keeps the shape light at the nape.

## 1. What Makes a Bob "A-Line"

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/a-line-bob/a-line-bob-01.webp" alt="Side profile of a classic A-line bob showing the single angled perimeter line running from the short nape down to longer points below the chin" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The A-line is the angle of the perimeter — the bottom edge of the cut. The hair is shortest at the nape and gets progressively longer as the edge travels forward, ending in the longest points at the front, usually between the jaw and the collarbone. Seen from the side, the bottom edge is one straight, diagonal line. There are no stacked layers building the shape — the silhouette is drawn entirely by that single sloping edge.

How steep the angle is changes the whole read. A subtle A-line drops the front only about an inch below the back — the angle is there, but the bob still reads soft and roughly even, and it grows out gracefully. A moderate angle gives roughly two inches of difference and is the classic version most people picture. A dramatic A-line drops the front three inches or more, ending in sharp points at or below the collarbone while the nape sits high and short. The steeper the angle, the more graphic the cut — and the faster regrowth blurs it.

## 2. Classic A-Line Bob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/a-line-bob/a-line-bob-02.webp" alt="Woman with a classic A-line bob, chestnut hair cut on a moderate forward angle with the front points resting at chin level, smooth glossy finish" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The classic is the moderate version: nape-length at the back, chin-length points at the front, a centre or just-off-centre part, and a smooth finish that lets the perimeter line do all the talking.

Wear it straight or with the ends turned very slightly under. A classic A-line is at its best when the surface is calm, because any wave or bend interrupts the diagonal. If your hair has natural movement, the wavy version further down is the more honest fit.

## 3. Subtle A-Line Bob

The subtle version keeps the front only about an inch longer than the back, so the angle reads as a gentle forward sweep rather than a statement.

This is the most wearable angle for most people and the kindest to grow out — when a gentle line softens, it just becomes a regular bob, not a mistake. It is the right first step if you like the shape but do not want a high-maintenance perimeter.

## 4. Dramatic A-Line Bob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/a-line-bob/a-line-bob-03.webp" alt="Woman with a dramatic A-line bob, sleek black hair cut on a steep angle from a high short nape to long sharp front points below the collarbone" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

The dramatic A-line pushes the angle to three inches of difference or more: the back sits high and close at the nape while the front points plunge to the collarbone or beyond. The silhouette is sharp, fashion-led and unmistakably deliberate.

Know what you are signing up for. A steep angle is the least forgiving cut in this list: the perimeter is the entire design, so it needs to be worn smooth, and the line starts softening within weeks. Love it, and budget for regular trims and daily smoothing.

## 5. A-Line Lob

The lob version keeps the same forward angle on a longer canvas — back around the shoulders, front points at or below the collarbone. Spread over more hair, the angle reads as polish rather than drama.

It is also the most forgiving long option: the front points are heavy enough to sit where they are put, and the longer back keeps the perimeter readable for longer between trims.

## 6. A-Line Bob with Full Fringe

A full, straight fringe changes the geometry: the long front points still angle down the sides, but the fringe closes the forehead and turns the outline into a frame on three sides. It works best on the classic moderate angle, where fringe and points stay in balance.

Be honest about the double upkeep. The fringe needs its own trims on its own schedule — if it grows shaggy while the perimeter stays crisp, the cut looks unfinished rather than relaxed. Two crisp lines or none.

## 7. A-Line Bob with Side Part

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/a-line-bob/a-line-bob-04.webp" alt="Woman with an A-line bob worn with a deep side part, honey blonde hair sweeping across the forehead with the longer front point falling along the jaw" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Moving the part to the side redistributes the angle: the heavy side sweeps across and falls in a longer curtain. The asymmetry flatters most faces and softens the formality of the classic version without touching the perimeter itself.

It is also a useful styling lever: a deep side part adds lift at the root on the heavy side, which fine hair benefits from.
## 8. A-Line Bob for Fine Hair

Fine hair and a one-length perimeter are natural allies: with no layers removing weight, every strand contributes to the line, and the ends look as thick as the hair allows. The angle then adds the shape fine hair struggles to hold with layering. Our guide to [bob haircuts for fine hair](/hairstyles/bangs-bobs/bob-haircuts-for-fine-hair/) covers the wider fine-hair bob family.

Keep the angle moderate and the front at or above chin length. Longer front points on fine hair separate and string out at the ends, which breaks the clean edge the cut exists for.

## 9. A-Line Bob for Thick Hair

Thick hair gives the A-line its fullest silhouette — the perimeter is dense, the angle unmistakable. The risk is the opposite of fine hair's: too much weight at the front points makes them hang heavy, and the back can bulk out where it curves over the nape.

The fix is invisible work, not visible layers: a stylist can remove weight from underneath the surface while leaving the perimeter untouched. Ask for the line to stay one-length and the weight to come out from beneath.

## 10. Curly A-Line Bob

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/a-line-bob/a-line-bob-05.webp" alt="Woman with a curly A-line bob, defined dark curls cut shorter at the back and falling longer toward the front, the angle visible as a soft diagonal through the curl pattern" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

An A-line can be cut on curly hair, but be clear-eyed about what survives: the angle reads softer. Curls do not lie along a straight diagonal, so instead of one clean line you get a stepped, sloping shape — shorter curls at the back, longer ones falling forward. The direction of the cut is still visible; the crispness is not.

Spring changes it day to day. Humidity, shrinkage and how the curls were dried all move the apparent length of the front points, so the back-to-front difference looks steeper some days and barely there on others. Cut it dry, in its natural state, and choose a moderate angle — a dramatic one asks for a precision curl behaviour will not cooperate with.

## 11. Wavy A-Line Bob

Loose waves sit between the straight and curly versions: the angle still reads, because the hair falls in one general direction, but the line is relaxed rather than ruled.

Style it by enhancing the wave everywhere except the last inch. Keeping the very ends straighter preserves a hint of the perimeter, while bending the ends into the wave makes the angle disappear entirely.

## 12. A-Line Bob with Balayage

<figure class="article-image"><img src="/images/articles/a-line-bob/a-line-bob-06.webp" alt="Back three-quarter view of an A-line bob with balayage, brunette hair lightening toward the long front points so the colour follows the downward angle of the cut" width="896" height="1200" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /></figure>

Colour can be made to follow the cut: balayage placed to lighten gradually toward the front points draws a second, softer angle over the cut one, and the eye travels down the line exactly as the shape intends. It is the most effective colour pairing for this cut because it reinforces rather than decorates.
Keep the lightness concentrated at the front and low on the head. Bright pieces at the short back fight the design — they pull attention to the highest, shortest point of a cut meant to lead forward and down.
## 13. Chin-Length A-Line Bob

The shortest version keeps the front points right at the chin and the back just below the nape — a compact angle, closer to the subtle end of the scale (the [French bob](/hairstyles/bangs-bobs/french-bob-comeback/) is its blunt, even cousin).

Short lengths show the angle honestly and grow out kindly, and chin-length points are the most flattering frame for a defined jaw. What you give up is versatility: there is not enough length to tie back, and the style lives or dies on the perimeter being kept tidy.

## A-Line vs Stacked vs Inverted Bob

These three names are used loosely in salons, sometimes for the same photograph, so here are the precise definitions. An **A-line bob** is a length angle only: the perimeter is cut in one length, shortest at the back and longest at the front, with no graduation layers building the shape. The silhouette comes entirely from where the hair ends.

A **[stacked bob](/hairstyles/bangs-bobs/stacked-bob-haircut/)** builds its shape differently: graduated layers cut close at the nape and stacked on each other, building rounded volume at the back. A stacked bob usually also runs longer at the front, so the two can look similar in a front-on photo — but turn both around and the difference is obvious: the A-line's back is a smooth one-length slope, the stacked bob's a stepped, layered build. If your priority is fullness at the back, stacked is your cut; if it is a clean swinging line, A-line is.

**Inverted bob** is the loose salon synonym. Most of the time it simply means the same angled shape as an A-line, and the words are used interchangeably. Sometimes, though, an inverted bob quietly includes light stacking at the nape, which makes it a hybrid. If a stylist uses the word, the useful question is not which name they prefer but whether they plan to graduate the back. The full [bob haircuts](/hairstyles/bangs-bobs/bob-haircuts/) pillar maps the whole family, and the [Italian bob](/hairstyles/bangs-bobs/italian-bob/) shows the softer direction a bob takes when the line is deliberately relaxed.

## Who It Suits

The angle draws the eye forward and down, which makes the A-line one of the more flattering bob shapes for round faces: the long front points sit either side of the cheeks, the diagonal breaks up width, and the face reads longer and narrower than it is. Square faces suit it too, provided the front points land below the jaw rather than exactly on it, where they would underline the jaw's corners.

Oval and heart-shaped faces can wear any steepness. Long faces should stay at the subtle end — a steep angle adds still more vertical emphasis, and a chin-length, gentler version keeps the balance. One honest condition applies to everyone: the front points are the design, so the perimeter has to be kept crisp. If you will not trim on schedule, choose the subtle angle, which forgives; the dramatic one does not.

## Styling the Angle

Blow-dry in the direction the hair is meant to fall — forward and down — with a paddle brush for the sleek versions, so each side dries along its diagonal rather than being coaxed there afterwards. A flat iron passed through in the same direction sharpens the line; curve it under very slightly at the ends if you want the points to sit against the jaw instead of kicking out.

One habit quietly undoes the whole cut: tucking the front points behind your ears. It breaks the long line at exactly the point the angle was built to create, and from the side the bob reads as an ordinary even cut with a gap in it. Finish with a light hand — heavy oils make the front points separate, and separated points are a broken line.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between an A-line bob and a stacked bob?

An A-line bob is defined by its length line: the hair is cut in one length, shortest at the back and longest at the front, with no layers building the shape. A stacked bob uses graduated layers stacked at the nape to build rounded volume at the back, and usually runs longer at the front as well. From the back, the A-line shows one smooth sloping edge, while the stacked bob shows visible stepped layers.
### Does an A-line bob suit a round face?

Yes, it is one of the more flattering bob shapes for a round face. The longer front points fall either side of the cheeks and the diagonal line draws the eye down, making the face read longer and narrower. A moderate angle with points at or below the chin works better than a very short version.

### How steep should the angle of an A-line bob be?

A subtle angle drops the front about an inch below the back and is the most wearable, forgiving option. A moderate angle of around two inches is the classic version most people picture. A dramatic angle of three inches or more looks striking but needs smooth styling and frequent trims, because the strong line softens quickly as the hair grows.

### Can an A-line bob work on curly hair?

Yes, but the result is a softer interpretation of the cut. Curls do not lie along a straight diagonal, so the angle appears as a general slope — shorter at the back, longer at the front — rather than one crisp line. It should be cut dry in its natural curl pattern, and a moderate angle holds up better than a dramatic one because shrinkage changes the apparent lengths day to day.

### How often does an A-line bob need a trim?

A subtle or moderate A-line typically needs a trim every six to eight weeks to keep the perimeter tidy. A dramatic angle needs attention sooner, around every four to six weeks, because the steep line is the entire design and regrowth blurs it quickly.

### Is an A-line bob hard to style?

No, it is one of the simpler bobs to style, because the one-length cut falls into shape with a straightforward blow-dry in a forward, downward direction. A flat iron sharpens the line when you want it sleek. The main thing to avoid is tucking the front points behind your ears, which breaks the angled line the cut is built around.
